## Problem Statement

The founder wants to release naym and make money on it — not defer the decision. His framing: "I want to release the app, and somehow make some money on it... whichever is more likely to make me money," comparing a flat ~$1/€1 price against in-app purchases.

## What Makes This Cool

Selling "go again, bigger" as a second ritual after the first reveal already paid off emotionally — instead of a locked size button before either partner has swiped a single name — turns the monetization moment into an extension of the app's own "whoa" moment instead of an interruption of it. The codebase already treats "another round" as a first-class emotional beat (`armRound2`, the decoy-padded round-2 deck for zero-match reveals). That precedent is what makes the *idea* natural — but it's the UI shape being reused (a post-reveal CTA offering more), not the round-2 deck-building math itself: round 2 decoy-pads the union of an already-completed session's own swipes, while a mega deck blends fresh names across origins — different server-side logic entirely, sharing only the "offer another round" moment.

**Correction from adversarial review — what "bigger" can actually mean.** Every deck file (`data/decks/*.json`) has exactly 50 names, matching `MAX_DECK_SIZE`. A single-origin deck of 75/100 is not buildable today without authoring 25-50 new curated names per origin — a real content cost this doc originally glossed over. The buildable path is different: `buildSessionDeck` already supports blending multiple origins (`lib/decks.ts`), and all five decks' ids are globally unique (enforced by `test/decks.test.ts`), so blending all five origins gives a pool of up to 250 names. A "mega deck" is therefore a **blended, cross-origin deck above today's 50-name ceiling** (a provisional working number: 100 names, blended across all five origins) — not a bigger single-origin deck. This requires raising the size ceiling specifically for a purchased session (a new paid-tier maximum, distinct from the public `MIN_DECK_SIZE`/`MAX_DECK_SIZE` everyone else uses), not authoring new content.

## Constraints

- Same architecture invariants as always (blindness boundary, monotonic sync, pinned deck snapshot) — untouched by this design.
- "Fun side project, no multi-quarter rewrites" still applies, inherited from the original app-store design doc.
- **Corrected mid-session**: in-app purchase support is NOT required to release to either app store. A free app or a flat-paid app with zero IAP is fully releasable. Apple's native-IAP-only rule applies narrowly: it only governs how you sell a digital unlock *from inside an app already distributed through their store* — it is not a precondition for releasing at all.
- The founder's actual goal, confirmed after that correction: "release" specifically means the App Store / Play Store, not just "live on the web" (naym is already live and free at naym.vercel.app). He chose to build this purchase as native IAP once the app-store shell exists, rather than ship a web-only interim version — an informed, deliberate choice, not a misunderstanding.

## Premises

1. For a brand-new, zero-review app, free-to-download + optional IAP is very likely to earn more total revenue than a flat upfront price. Confirmed via web research: paid-upfront apps are under ~5% of both app stores and contribute even less to total downloads; the large majority of aggregate app-store revenue flows through free-to-download apps monetizing via IAP/subscriptions. (Some other cited "paid converts 5-8x better" stats trace back to subscription hard-paywall-vs-freemium benchmarking — a different funnel — and were discounted as noisy.) — **Agreed.**
2. The real blocker to shipping an IAP was never payment engineering — it was that no specific paid feature existed yet ("in-app purchase options" was still just a concept). Resolved: founder picked a concrete idea. — **Agreed.**
3. IAP does not, in general, require the Capacitor app-store wrap — a Stripe-based web unlock could ship independent of any store timeline. The founder considered this and still chose to gate the purchase on the store-submission project, because for him "release" means the app stores specifically. — **Explicitly chosen (Approach B), after the App-Store/IAP misconception above was corrected — re-affirmed, not a mistake.**
4. The unlock should be a post-reveal "go again, bigger" offer, not a locked preset button on the pre-ritual landing page. Surfaced by an independent second-opinion review; the founder agreed once it was pointed out that a locked size button is itself a small paywall on the one screen meant to feel inviting. — **Agreed.**

Key points, trimmed to what transfers to the chosen approach (its original technical sketch assumed the rejected Approach A's Stripe path, since native IAP wasn't yet decided at that point):

## Approaches Considered

### Approach A: Web-only Stripe Checkout, post-reveal unlock — rejected
Ships this week, no store review needed, ~2-3 days of isolated work reusing existing session/store code. Rejected because the founder's actual goal is app-store release specifically, and he was fine trading immediate revenue for a single native purchase path instead of building one now and rebuilding it later as native IAP.

### Approach B: Wait for the Capacitor app-store wrap, build native IAP directly — CHOSEN
No throwaway work, one canonical purchase path. Depends entirely on the separate, still-unbuilt, unscoped Capacitor project (prior design doc's Next Steps #3-6) — makes zero revenue until that ships. Chosen anyway, deliberately, because "release" means the app stores to this founder.

### Approach C: Ship the bigger deck free first, validate demand, monetize later — rejected
Zero risk, immediate ship, tests real demand before spending effort on purchase plumbing. **Correction from adversarial review**: this was originally rejected for "earning nothing in the interim" — but the chosen Approach B *also* earns nothing in the interim and adds an unscoped, untimed dependency on top, so revenue timing doesn't actually favor B over C. The real rejection reason is Approach C doesn't move the founder toward his stated goal of an app-store release at all (it's a pure web feature) — that's the reason that actually holds.

## Recommended Approach

**Approach B**, per the founder's explicit, twice-confirmed choice. The corrected understanding that matters here: this is not "IAP is required to release," it's "release means the app stores to me, and I'm willing to wait for that project rather than ship an interim web version." That reframes what actually needs to happen next — see Next Steps.

## Open Questions

- Does the free deck-size cap stay at today's `MAX_DECK_SIZE = 50`, or drop (e.g. to 35) so that the blended mega deck (this doc provisionally uses 100 — the exact target is still open) becomes the paid tier? A product call, not a code call — needs deciding before `lib/deck-manifest.ts` changes.
- **Price point.** The Problem Statement anchored on "~$1/€1" for a flat price, but once the doc pivots to IAP, no price is attached to the mega-deck unlock specifically. Still open, alongside the deck-size ceiling above.
- **What happens if the other partner's browser sees the unlock offer, not the buyer's?** (Promoted from Cross-Model Perspective — unresolved.) Naym's whole model is two tokens, two browsers; a purchase made on A's device needs to unlock the *session*, not just that one browser, or B never sees the bigger deck A paid for. Needs a real answer before any purchase UI is designed.
- **Consumable vs. non-consumable, and what "persistence" actually costs.** A non-consumable purchase's persistence doesn't require naym to invent its own login/accounts system — native store billing already tracks entitlement per platform account (StoreKit 2's `Transaction.currentEntitlements`, Play Billing's `queryPurchasesAsync`), so the app can just ask the store "does this device/account own this?" on launch. The real, smaller cost the review correctly flagged: naym's session model (`lib/session.ts`) has no concept of "device" or "install" today, only anonymous 30-day-TTL session tokens — some plumbing is needed to check that native entitlement at the right point in the flow (before a deck is created) and connect it to a fresh session. Moderate work, not a new architecture, but real and unscoped today.
- **What happens to the free web app** (`naym.vercel.app`) once the mega-deck exists only as native IAP? Working assumption: the web app keeps its current free experience unchanged (capped at `MAX_DECK_SIZE`); the paid tier is native-app-only for now. This is a deliberate asymmetry, not an oversight — revisit if that feels wrong once the native app actually exists.
- No timeline exists yet for the Capacitor wrap itself — this document doesn't create one. It does make explicit that this project is now the literal critical path to any naym revenue, not merely a "nice to have" for app-store discovery as originally framed. See Success Criteria — this is now a hard prerequisite, not an aside.

## Success Criteria

- **Hard prerequisite, not an aside**: the Capacitor app-store wrap gets an owner and a timeline. Under the chosen approach, this feature — and all naym revenue — is completely blocked until that happens; this document does not create that timeline, it only makes the dependency explicit.
- The native shell integrates a purchase bridge (e.g. RevenueCat, or a Capacitor/Cordova IAP plugin) for StoreKit/Play Billing — this is a real prerequisite tool decision, not implicit in "wrap the app."
- Once the shell and purchase bridge exist, the "go again, bigger" post-reveal offer ships as a new UI state (reusing the post-reveal CTA *pattern* round 2 established, not its deck-building code — see "What Makes This Cool" above), without touching the free ritual, the blindness boundary, or any existing test.
- The product decisions made in this session (post-reveal moment not a landing-page paywall; a blended cross-origin deck not new curated content; mega-deck-size as the specific unlock) carry forward without needing to be re-litigated when that work actually starts.

## Next Steps

1. No code changes now — this document records the monetization decision so it doesn't need re-deciding later.
2. The actual unblocking action is the Capacitor wrap itself (prior design doc's Next Steps #3: wrap the app, get a debug build running on a real device end-to-end). It has no owner or timeline yet; under this session's decision, it is now the precondition for any revenue, which may change how the founder prioritizes it. **Cross-reference**: `docs/designs/same-device-pairing-and-app-stores.md` already flags real Guideline 4.2.3 ("minimum functionality") rejection risk for the Capacitor wrap in general. This doc's working assumption — not a claim the sibling doc itself makes — is that gating a real purchase behind that same WebView likely raises that scrutiny further, not lowers it. Factor this into that project's own native-touch decisions, not just this one.
3. When that work starts, resolve the open pricing/product questions above (deck-size ceiling, price point, partner-attribution, consumable vs. non-consumable), plus the purchase bridge choice from Success Criteria, before touching App Store Connect / Play Console.
4. Decide restore-purchase and refund handling — required by both stores for non-consumable IAP, not optional polish — as part of that same setup pass.
5. Build the post-reveal "go again, bigger" UI state (in `components/Reveal.tsx` / `lib/session.ts`, alongside but not reusing the existing round-2 deck-building code) once the native shell and purchase bridge exist.
